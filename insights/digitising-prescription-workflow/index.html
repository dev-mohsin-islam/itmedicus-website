What to consider when digitising a doctor’s prescription workflow

ITmedicus Delivery Team 388 words Healthcare Technology

A prescription is the most-used document in a doctor’s working day. It is also the place where digitisation projects most often stall, because the printed pad is not merely a document — it is a small system that already works, and every part of it exists for a reason.

Start with the actual sequence of events

Before proposing a screen, we sit with the doctor and the assistant and record what happens between a patient arriving and a prescription being handed over. Where does the file come from? Who writes what? When is the assistant interrupted? Which step is the bottleneck on a busy evening? The answers usually reveal that the valuable digitisation is not the prescription itself but the queue in front of it.

The assistant is a primary user

Many prescription tools are designed for the doctor and then handed to an assistant to operate. That mismatch shows up as extra keystrokes, duplicated entry and quiet workarounds. If an assistant will enter patient details, the workflow must be designed for speed of entry with a queue of waiting patients in mind — keyboard-first where possible, with defaults that remove repeated typing.

  • Design for interruption: a chamber consultation is constantly paused and resumed.
  • Assume the assistant, not the doctor, types most of the text.
  • Keep a printable output that looks credible to a pharmacist.
  • Plan for the day the network is down — the queue cannot stop.

Never break the paper fallback on day one

A clinic that has used a printed pad for twenty years will not accept a system that can fail to produce a prescription. The transition works when the digital path is reliable and the paper path remains available without drama. We treat print output as a first-class feature, not a legacy concession.

Measure the time it actually saves

The claim that digitisation saves time should be measurable. We agree a baseline before go-live — average minutes per patient, number of repeat entries, corrections per prescription — and compare after a month of real use. If the numbers do not move, the design is wrong, not the users.

Prescription software is a workflow product wearing a clinical interface. Treated that way, it can remove a great deal of daily friction. Treated as a form to be replicated on screen, it usually adds some.
